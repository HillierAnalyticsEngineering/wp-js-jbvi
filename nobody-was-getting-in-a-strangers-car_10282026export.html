Where I keep starting from

Nobody was getting in a stranger's car

Before ride-hailing existed, that was the objection to it, and it was a good one. Your parents told you not to. Ride-hailing needed a social rule to flip before it could work, and nothing written up to that point said it was about to. Hand that idea to a machine and ask it to score the odds, and it calls the thing far-fetched: right about the record, wrong about what came next. Ask that same machine to help you think the idea through instead, and you get something else entirely, which turns out to be most of the story.

The car at the curb was thought up on a call with the authors, not taken from the paper. Captions below mark the paper's own examples.

Founders and the people who fund them now ask tools like ChatGPT and Claude what a new business idea is worth.

“AI-mediated plausibility regimes,” a theory paper in this journal by Farsan Madjdi and Bernd Wurth of the University of Glasgow, pins down what that answer measures and how much weight it should carry.

I got the authors on a call. Nine ideas came out of it, each a section below.

How I ended up here

My company asked me to get up to speed on generative AI. I had a background in NLP and machine learning, studied properly anyway, and came out with a certification. Now I build AI chat prototypes at work and teach workshops at the incubator where I keep an office.

I was also getting uneasy about how often I reached for it. Not for code. For thinking. Framing an idea. Pressure-testing it. Pitching it. I run a venture of my own, and I'd stopped having the awkward first draft of a thought, because something was always there to tidy it up first. Was that making me sharper, or just making me sound like everyone else?

Then I read this paper. Its authors came into the university from industry, and one works in simulation and system dynamics, where I came from.

So I got them on a call, with two questions of my own.

One. I'm chasing non-dilutive funding, and I use these tools to sharpen the applications. Is that helping my odds or quietly hurting them?

Two. I use them while I'm thinking, too. Is that making me better at finding something genuinely new, or walking me back toward whatever everyone already believes?

Both got answered, mostly sideways. The answers run through what follows, and I collect them at the end.

      Familiar, and it shows its work

      What comes back from the score

      Looks great

      High confidence. Which makes sense, because a machine that learned from a million business plans is going to feel good about a business plan that looks like a million business plans.

      Looks risky

      Low confidence, flagged. Not because there's anything weak in the folder, but because the folder has no relatives. Hard to recognize gets read as unlikely to work.

      The short version

      Ask it to judge, and what comes back is the past's opinion

      Authors Farsan Madjdi and Bernd Wurth argue that a system trained on history hands out a kind of vote of confidence. How much that vote matters depends on whether it sits beside a decision or quietly becomes one. Their name for the setup, tools plus institutions plus habits, is a plausibility regime.

      My everyday version, not the paper's: a mortgage application. The bank isn't asking whether you'll pay it back. It's asking how closely you resemble people who did. Someone self-employed with a better income and a lumpier record scores worse, and everyone knows the score is a proxy, right up until it becomes the decision.

      One property, two opposite mistakes, depending on the job you hand it. Used to invent, it can talk up ideas that merely sound right. Used to screen, it can talk down anything it hasn't seen. The authors call these risks, not certainties, and the tool isn't the villain.

      Farsan kept returning to what that leaves the founder holding.

      … entrepreneurial judgment is becoming very important, precisely because the relevant evidence does not yet exist.Farsan Madjdi, in conversation
      The first thing I asked them

      Some questions arrive before the evidence can

      I pushed them on this first, because it's the first thing anyone asks about the paper. Wurth walked through why the usual method doesn't reach.

      … the literature on experiments is very thin, and just because it's difficult to do.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      The reason is structural. You can't rerun a funding round, and no two applications, committees or years line up cleanly enough to compare. Section three gets into why, with jars.

      A familiar shape if you've worked on any big messy system. Nobody runs a controlled trial on a city's transport network either. You build the argument, look for the traces it would leave, and go carefully. That's what this paper does.

      One thing to keep straight: when the paper says the machine gets it wrong, it means it treated I don't recognize this as evidence of this won't work. A claim about the reasoning, not the outcome.

      Two — what's actually being measured

      It's answering a different question than the one you asked.

      You want to know if this will work. It's telling you how much this looks like things that worked. For a familiar venture those two are close enough to be useful. The further you get from the familiar, the further apart they drift.

      The symmetry author Farsan Madjdi kept coming back to

      The same machinery that rewards resemblance punishes its absence

      There's no separate channel for "unprecedented and good." Two ventures get low marks and high marks for reasons that have nothing to do with each other, and the output looks the same either way.

      Something that could look plausible … resembles what has worked before, while another venture can look implausible simply because there is little precedent for it.Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      The paper says this plainly: on well-trodden ground, resemblance is a reasonable thing to measure. It's real information about a market that exists. The trouble starts when it gets read as information about one that doesn't.

      In plain terms

      Imagine asking someone very well read who has never met your customers. You get a confident answer assembled from what other people wrote down. Worth something. Not worth one honest conversation with a customer, and much easier to get, which is the whole trouble.

      This landed on me directly. I'd been reading the model's answer about my own ideas as a verdict. It was a distance measurement, and I'd confused the two for months.

      Which raises the obvious follow-up: if distance is all it can measure, what exactly is it that can't be measured? Two jars.

      Three — two kinds of not knowing

      There's not knowing the answer, and then there's the answer not existing yet.

      This distinction is doing most of the work in the paper. I found it easier to picture than to define, so here are two jars.

      One jar, two kinds of not knowing

      Drag, count, then draw

      Drag a marble down into the jar. Drop one outside and it rolls away. The jar holds ten.

      chance of drawing maroon 50% 3 of 6 marbles
      chance of drawing navy 50% 3 of 6 marbles

      Add or remove marbles and the odds follow.

      — no draws yet

      That's risk, and it's the easy one.

      You don't know which marble comes out, but you know what's in there, so the odds fall out of counting. Loads of business decisions look like this. How many customers churn next quarter, with three years of history behind it. Which of three sites the new shop should go in.

      Author Bernd Wurth used that second one himself as an example of where these tools genuinely earn their money.

      If you have data sets about population density, what other stores are there and everything, AI will easily find you the top three locations …Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      That's two weeks saved, and nobody in this argument has a problem with any of it.

      That's uncertainty, and it's a different animal.

      Same jar. You built it a minute ago. Now you can't see in, and here's the part that matters: something keeps topping it up while you play, so the mix drifts. Your last twenty draws tell you nothing about the next one.

      That's the friction. No number to find, so nothing to be right about. You can't compute the odds or audit the decision afterward, because the thing you decided against has already changed. The economist Frank Knight described this a century ago, and a lot of new ventures live there.

      Ask in 2007 whether people will let strangers sleep in their spare room, and you aren't facing an estimation problem. The answer hadn't been decided yet. Airbnb's founders didn't find it. They built it, coaxing people into trusting each other until the whole thing felt normal.

      Airbnb is one of the paper's own examples, p. 4.
      Why "just get better data" doesn't rescue you

      Medicine gets a control group

      Drug trials work because you can hold a group back and compare. That's how a fuzzy question turns into a number you can act on.

      … that's why you do RCTs, like a gold standard in medicine, because you establish the counterfactual.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      An RCT is a randomised controlled trial: split people into two groups at random, treat one and not the other, and the gap between them is your answer. The counterfactual is the group you held back, the version of events where you didn't do the thing. It's the whole basis of knowing that a drug works.

      Funding decisions don't

      You see something once and your judgment has already moved. Present it differently and you get a different answer. No two founders are the same input, and no two panels are either.

      You can't give an investor the same venture over and over.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      So the founder has to spend money before anyone can know. That's the job.

      … entrepreneurs have to commit resources before the relevant evidence exists.Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      The crowded peak

      Plenty like it, and the data knows how those went.

      What it looks like
      Stable, repeated patterns that are well covered in what's been written. You really can work out how it's likely to go from things that already happened.
      Which tools
      Prediction, scoring, forecasting.
      What goes wrong
      Mild overconfidence. A close comparison gets treated as a promise rather than a hint.
      Who ends up funded
      Ventures that fit the existing criteria, efficiently. Little effect on the range of what gets backed.
      What to do
      Use it as an aid. Show how confident it is and how close the comparison really was. Flag where the match is only partial.
      The shoulder

      Genuinely new, but you can still find something to hold it against.

      What it looks like
      Real novelty, with partial analogies available. Comparable markets, visible demand, something to reason from.
      Which tools
      Generative models, analytics, market intelligence.
      What goes wrong
      False positives. Something that hangs together gets mistaken for something that's understood.
      Who ends up funded
      More ideas get considered, but attention can drift back to familiar recombinations rather than departures from them.
      What to do
      Treat what it produces as a provocation, not an endorsement. Make somebody establish the feasibility separately before anyone commits money.
      The thin tail

      Nothing like it, and somebody has to make the call.

      What it looks like
      Whether it works depends on experiments nobody has run, rules that don't exist yet, or a category that has to be built. This is where the rogue ideas live.
      Which tools
      Prediction and screening, where the miscalibration is worst. Generative models are usable here only for provocation, never for a verdict.
      What goes wrong
      False negatives. Hard to recognize gets read as unlikely to work.
      Who ends up funded
      Where the score gets the last word, not these. They lose credibility and face a higher bar, and the tail thins out.
      What to do
      The score doesn't decide. Carve out room in your screening for things with no comparison set, and put a human argument next to every number.
      Cutting across all three

      And then the score stops advising and starts deciding.

      This is the dashed line, and it's the paper's sketch of a risk rather than a forecast. Same ideas, same budget, different shape. The peak grows because coherent-sounding plans get talked up, the tail thins because unfamiliar ones get talked down, and the total underneath barely moves. Which is why it's easy to miss.

      What it looks like
      Scores baked into funding, accelerator intake, hiring or whatever gets shown to whom, whatever kind of venture is being judged.
      Which tools
      Rankings, recommenders, automated screening, decision-support pipelines.
      What goes wrong
      Everything above, amplified. The number stands in for the judgment instead of feeding it.
      Who ends up funded
      The recognizable, across every category at once, unless something is deliberately protecting the range.
      What to do
      Cap what the score is allowed to weigh. Require a human to sign off on any low-precedent rejection. Audit the spread of what you funded, once a year.

      The thing to take from this

      Everything in the paper hangs off one measurement

      How much has already been written about the thing you're proposing. That's the shelf gap from earlier, the x-axis here, and what sorts the four cases. It says nothing about whether your idea is good. How much it decides depends on how much weight the room gives the score.

      The authors treat this as a risk, not a rule. The symptom isn't a smaller budget. It's that everything you fund starts to look alike. Which brings up the obvious objection.

      Five — the obvious objection

      Somebody still has to say no.

      Here's the pushback I'd expect at a board table, and the reason I don't think it lands where people think it does.

      The objection

      Selection is the job

      Money runs out. Every round turns down most of what comes in, and it always did. If the unfamiliar applications get turned down more often than the familiar ones, that might be nothing more sinister than a committee doing what committees are for.

      There's a stronger version of it too, and you'll hear it in the room. Most new ventures fail. Most of the strange ones fail harder. A screen that's tough on things nobody has tried is being sensible, not narrow-minded.

      Two problems with that

      The first is uncomfortable, and I only noticed it while writing this up. "Most of them would have failed anyway" assigns odds to the exact set where the argument is that you can't. Nobody has that denominator. Nobody tracks what happens to unfamiliar ideas that get turned down, and the fate of the never-funded isn't observable anyway. The claim feels like evidence and is really a feeling of familiarity, the same move the machine makes.

      The second: the paper isn't disputing any particular no. It's asking whether the reason behind the no has quietly become the same reason everywhere. One committee reaching for what it recognizes is judgment. Every committee reaching for the same thing, because they consult tools trained on the same past, is something else.

      What I'd actually do about it

      You can't run the study. You can instrument your own room.

      The paper closes by naming the research it wants done: watch whether money drifts toward familiar-looking ventures, and whether the spread of what gets funded narrows where these tools are built in. A hard study to run, for all the reasons above.

      You don't need a literature to see your own desk. Log the decisions. Note when a human overrides the score, and why. Check the spread of what you funded once a year. That's paperwork, not a research program, and it answers the question for the one room you control. The draft policy at the bottom does exactly that.

      Seven — advice, or a locked door

      Everything changes when the score stops being a suggestion.

      Same output. Different spot in the process. Completely different consequences.

      Where it sits

      Sitting beside you

      It widens the search. Author Farsan Madjdi took the paradox head on: the range of ideas you consider genuinely grows, through combinations you wouldn't have reached. What shifts is which ones feel worth taking seriously.

      Sitting at the door

      The same output inside an investment committee or an accelerator's intake becomes the thing that decides, rather than a thing that informs.

      … you're being pushed towards the consensus …Bernd Wurth, in conversation
      And consensus isn't neutral ground

      The past is the training set

      This is where the bias conversation stops being a separate topic. If evaluation has historically favored certain founders and certain ways of writing, that preference is sitting in the data.

      AI is trained on all the past.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      Asking nicely doesn't fix it

      Author Bernd Wurth's point is that telling a system to be fair doesn't make it fair, because the habit is in what it learned rather than in what you typed.

      Just by asking AI, you tell AI to be impartial. It's not automatically impartial because these biases are in there.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      So "pushes toward consensus" and "repeats old preferences" turn out to be the same behavior with two names, and an unusual founder can get caught by both at once.

      What happens when everyone's using it

      Every application gets better

      This is my first question, and the first half of the answer is yes. You use it to sharpen a grant application and your odds go up. I've felt that. Then Farsan asked the thing I hadn't.

      … if everyone else is doing it, what does that then actually mean? So where do we end then?Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      And the signal goes flat

      Once every application is buffed by the same sort of tool, buffing stops telling you anything. Bernd mentioned the parallel in research funding, where one proposal is to stop ranking above a quality bar and draw lots instead.

      … above that, it basically becomes a lottery because it's so difficult and there's so much variation, like who your reviewer is …Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      Hiring looks to be running the same experiment right now. Written by a machine, screened by a machine, and a stack of good candidates who all read the same.

      What a reviewer sees after a year of this

      Round one, before any of this. Three applications came in. Pick the strongest.

      Round two, a year later. Five came in. Pick the strongest.

      reads like what you already fundedreads like nothing you have seen
      Twenty runs of the next three years, one every two seconds. A simulation, not a finding. In the real thing you get one run and you only see the row you funded.

      The line the paper draws, in its own words

      None of this is automatic

      If you take one thing from the paper, take this. The authors don't argue these tools are restrictive by nature. The claim is conditional: it turns on what you use the tool for, how legible your venture is in prior data, and how much authority the number gets.

      This effect is conditional rather than inevitable, where AI outputs are treated as advisory, variance may be preserved or even widened, whereas where they become authoritative gatekeeping mechanisms they redistribute legitimacy toward historically validated trajectories.Madjdi and Wurth, p. 4

      Read the middle clause twice. Treated as advice, the variety of what gets funded may hold steady or even widen; the narrowing shows up at the door, not in the tool. The paper is just as direct the other way: where a venture is legible in prior data, AI “may usefully augment judgment,” and generative systems “may expand the range of ideas entrepreneurs consider.”Quoted phrases, p. 3. The conditional statement, p. 4.

      So my first answer: it helps, it helps everyone, and an advantage everyone holds cancels out. The last thing it does is close the gap between an application that reads well and one that reads badly. If your edge was writing better than the next person, that edge is going.

      Eight — the hard one

      So how do you tell brilliant from daft?

      This is the question I came into the call wanting answered. Both cases look identical from outside: no precedent, no comparison, nobody's done it. Neither author pretended it resolves cleanly, and I respected them more for saying so.

      What's left when the data can't help

      Evidence you go and make

      You can't run the trial, but you can run a pilot. A small group of real people who actually paid. Letters of intent. Recordings of customers describing the problem in their own words. None of that is in any training set, which is precisely why it's worth something.

      The people, and what they've already pulled off

      Author Farsan Madjdi turned the question back on the institutions, and it's the one I'd most want an incubator director to sit with.

      Should incubators, accelerators emphasize the idea that potential applicants come up with, or … the profile of applicants … people who show or have agency and who are capable of execution?Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      Now that anyone can have their idea written up beautifully, proof that someone gets things done is proof a machine didn't generate.

      From the interview

      The bit where somebody just has to have the idea

      Underneath all of this is the leap: coming up with the hypothesis before there's anything to check it against. You can't calculate your way to an idea nobody's had. Somebody has to have it, and then the testing starts.

      You do a small version of it constantly. Your car makes a noise on the highway and before any diagnostic runs, you already have a guess about what it is. The guess came from somewhere, it isn't a calculation, and the mechanic will confirm or kill it later.

      The two ledges above are the difference in miniature. Left, a cab you can book ahead: easy to explain to a committee, harder than it looks because the market already exists. Right, the thing that worked, which needed a rule about strangers to change first. You can walk to the left idea. You have to jump to the right one.

      Farsan's example was Steve Jobs at Xerox PARC, the research lab where much of the modern computer interface got built by a company that never worked out what it had. The technology was sitting there in the building. Seeing what it was for was a separate act entirely.

      His wider point was that the things that move a society along have rarely arrived with the evidence already assembled.

      It worked based on visions, on imaginations of entrepreneurs … They weren't in line with these plausibility signals and this expert consensus. These people actually had to go around it, or they even had to go against it.Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      Which raises something we chewed on for a while, and it's speculation rather than anybody's finding: how much one of these tools can help you spot something new might depend on how much you already know. It's not much use being hit on the head by an apple if you're not Newton.

      My second question, more or less answered. These tools hand you the adjacent thing, the ledge you can walk to, and they can widen the search. They can't make the jump for you. And if you don't carry enough of the domain to recognize the far ledge, a wider search just gives you more places to stand on the near one.

      A score tells you what the past makes of your idea. It can't tell you what the future will do with it.

      Nine — Monday morning

      Don't ask it for a verdict. Ask it for arguments.

      The practical suggestion from the interview, and then a draft policy you can scribble on.

      Where the suggestion came from

      Stop asking one machine one question

      Author Farsan Madjdi's instinct was to treat it the way you'd treat money.

      … potentially you would use like a portfolio in a way. It's kind of like … how you should invest.Farsan Madjdi, in conversation

      Author Bernd Wurth made it concrete by pointing at something a lot of us already do daily, in a completely different context.

      … when you use them for coding … they do the implementation, they do a review and then they typically do an adversarial review. So they purposely try and find errors.Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      Nobody ships code on one pass from one model. That's what most of us do when we ask a model about a business plan.

      Four reviews of one unfamiliar application

      Open each one

      Click to open

      Four passes
      Pass one

      Believe every word. Does it hold together?

      Do the claims agree with each other? Do the numbers agree with the claims? It's the cheapest genuinely useful thing these tools do, and it's a tidiness check. Passing means the application isn't muddled. That's all it means.

      Pass two

      Now assume everything they predict comes true.

      Push it as far as it goes. If the world moves the way they say it will, what is this thing? Are we funding it at the right size, or should we be doing a great deal more? This is the pass that stops you under-reacting to something big.

      Pass three

      Now try to knock it over.

      Bernd put it as a question you ask deliberately, one assumption at a time.

      What if individual assumptions don't hold? Do you still have a business, or is the whole thing falling apart?Bernd Wurth, in conversation

      In the Uber case that assumption is people getting into cars driven by strangers. Naming it tells you the cheap experiment you should run before you write a big cheque.

      Pass four

      Is this a new field, or a graveyard?

      There's nothing to compare it to. Fine. But is that because nobody has tried, or because plenty tried and nobody wrote it up? Opposite answers, same score. You have to ask this one on purpose, because nothing else in your process will. This pass is my addition, not theirs.

      And then

      Four reports, possibly all agreeing, and the call is still yours. If you still think there's something in it, that's not a bug. That's the part of the job nobody was ever going to do for you.

      Structure suggested by the authors, who pointed out that coding assistants already work this way: write it, review it, then attack it.
      What they're not saying

      Neither of them thinks you should stop using LLMs

      Both said so directly and more than once. The argument is about where the output sits, how much authority you hand it, and whether anyone has bothered to check what it can't see.

      … it's helpful to compare it to certain patterns, but that shouldn't be the only criteria.Bernd Wurth, in conversation
      One last thing

      The paper's acknowledgements say the authors used Claude to help with copyediting and checked every change themselves. Which is, more or less, the policy the paper recommends, applied to the paper.

      What I came away with

      I went in with two questions about my own work. Both answers were smaller, and more useful, than the ones I'd imagined.

      On whether these tools help my funding odds: yes, and so does everyone else's copy of them. The lift stops being an advantage the moment everyone has it. What survives is the part I can't outsource, so I've stopped polishing and started collecting: a pilot with people who paid, customers describing the problem in their own words, the assumption that would sink the whole thing written down before anyone asks.

      On whether they blunt my ability to find something new: not exactly, but they pull. Generous with the adjacent idea, quiet about the one nobody has tried. The quiet is easy to mistake for a verdict. It's a report on how much has been written, and for anything genuinely new that's close to nothing.

      So I've changed what I ask for. Not whether an idea is good, because it will say yes and I'll believe it, since I wanted to. I ask it to take the idea apart. Then I go find the thing the past couldn't tell me.

      The uneasy feeling hasn't gone. It's gotten more specific, which is the most I can ask of an hour with two people who've thought about this longer than I have. I know what I'm reaching for the tool to do now, and what it was never going to do.
