The sentence every designer dreads

It reminds me of something else

Two candid conversations on producer and consumer (mis)alignment in perceptions of product distinctiveness

A designer spends two years making something nobody has seen. A player picks up the box and says this is basically Catan. Same object, two answers, and only one of them decides whether the game sells.

How this one started

I got on a call with all four authors: Ryan Angus, Matt Barlow, Hyeonsuh Lee and Cameron Verhaal. They had asked 28 board game designers how distinctive their games were, then asked 127 experienced players the same thing about the same games. The two sets of answers barely related to each other.

About twenty minutes in, I said it reminded me of a friend I grew up with. Bobby Powell and I played board games together all through high school, at his house, with his family. His basement had a wall of games floor to ceiling. In four years we never had to play the same one twice.

Bobby founded CavernWire Games. He designs games now. I had heard him describe this exact frustration for years without either of us calling it research, so after the call I phoned him.

Half of what follows is the paper. The other half is Bobby, giving me the working practitioner's read: what these findings look like to someone who has to design a game, price it, pitch it and then sell it.

Where the design starts

An argument with one specific game

Author Hyeonsuh Lee walked me through the case that opens the paper. A designer played a game, wasn't satisfied with it, and set out to solve a specific problem in its mechanics. Every choice in his design points back at that one title.

Then playtesters told him his game reminded them of something else. Hyeonsuh said the designer was surprised, and then did the only thing available to him.

… he really had to go back and play that board game that was referenced by the consumers …Author Hyeonsuh Lee, in conversation

Whatever's already on their shelf

The general player in the study owns at least ten games, plays at least monthly, or both. That shelf is their entire frame of reference, assembled by birthdays, game nights and whatever the local store carried.

They run the new box against it in about four seconds. If something surfaces, that's the benchmark. If nothing surfaces, they don't think I'm missing context. They think this is new.

… you just look at this, and you're like, 'Oh, (it) looks legit.'Designer BG3, quoted in the paper, Table 5, p. 8
Digest — what the paper tests

There's an assumption buried in the theory

Optimal distinctiveness says a product should be different enough to stand out and familiar enough to make sense. Research has treated that as a dial the producer sets. Setting a dial only works if the other person reads the number you set, and nobody had checked that step with both sides in the room.

In plain terms

Think of a kitchen labelling a curry "medium." The cook sets that dial deliberately. The person eating decides what medium turned out to mean, and their verdict is the only one that reaches the review. Same dish, two readings, and nobody did anything wrong.

How the four dimensions get used

A deliberate split bet

Designers conform on one dimension to earn a hearing, then push hard on another. Author Hyeonsuh Lee described producers being intentional about it: novel on theme, common mechanics to keep the game playable. It's careful work, and it assumes a buyer who can tell the dimensions apart.

You need 200% just to get 100%.Designer BG4, quoted in the paper, Table 5, p. 7

One overall impression

Players rated the four dimensions separately and landed near the middle on all of them, whatever the designer intended. Only art tracked the designer at all, and art is the one thing you can judge without playing the game.

… the consumers are not that discriminate.Author Hyeonsuh Lee, in conversation
The one place they meet

Art. For every full point the designer moved, the typical player in the study moved about a sixth of a point. On the other three, nothing.

Which games come to mind

Niche

Designers benchmark against the games that taught them something. Sprawling, specialist, often out of print. That's the cost of expertise: the more you've played, the further your reference set drifts from your buyer's.

Digest — why tabletop makes it worse

Two things stack up

First, a board game is an experience good. What you're buying is a few hours at a table with other people, and the box can't convey that. Second, a game has four fairly independent dimensions. The designer knows which one carries the novelty. The buyer has no idea where to look, so the buyer averages.

In plain terms

Experience good: a mattress. You can read the tag all day and know nothing until you've slept on it for a month. Four dimensions: buying a car. The salesperson is excited about the suspension geometry. You're looking at the color.

Four — the blank

The most distinctive-looking games were the ones nobody could place.

This runs backwards from intuition, and it's the result a founder should sit with.

Reading a high novelty score

What you want it to mean

The design worked. The positioning landed. They saw it.

What it might mean

Naming a different game than the designer barely moved the score. Naming no game moved everything, mechanics most of all.

So part of what looks like novelty isn't recognition. It's a blank. The same score can mean I see what you did or I have no idea what I'm looking at, and from outside they're identical.

The problem reaches the researchers too

And the ratings themselves barely move. Whatever a designer intended, from 1 to 5, the typical player's number sits near 3. Experience narrows the gap. It does not close it.

Shelf three — how wide the gap gets

Narrowest. Look how narrow that isn't.

This is the tier the study comes closest to measuring. Even here, designers and players landed on the same reference game for two of the ten games tested. Four times out of five, an experienced buyer is measuring your game against something you never had in mind.

Who that actually was: 127 people who told an online survey they play at least monthly or own ten-plus games, rating games from a description. Not designers at a pitch table, not testers in a Protospiel room, not shoppers observed in a store. There is real crossover between that group and the convention crowd, and it is the closest proxy the study has for shelf three, but the two are not the same population.

Who the pitch gets built for

You test it on shelf three

Conventions, playtest groups, pitch events. All hobbyist rooms. The feedback shaping how a designer explains their game comes from the people whose shelf looks least like the market's.

You sell on shelves one and two

The study's players were already enthusiasts, monthly players or owners of ten-plus games. Even they mostly reached for Catan and Risk.

Whatever gap got measured among enthusiasts is the narrow version. It widens at the endcap.

Corroboration from a publisher

The same tier, described from a publisher's desk

David Blanchard, director of party and family games at The Op, has described the fight for mass retail in almost the same terms. At Walmart and Target, he says, a new party card game is up against sensations like Taco Cat Goat Cheese Pizza and Exploding Kittens, plus decade-old classics he calls ingrained in the collective subconscious: UNO, Phase 10, Skip-Bo, Dutch Blitz.

Why that matters here

A designer and a publisher, describing shelf one separately, land on nearly the same short list. That list is what a new game gets measured against, and it overlaps almost not at all with what a designer benchmarks against.

From Bobby — comparison is the native language

Hobbyist designers already work this way

Benchmarking against an existing game isn't a marketing afterthought for them. It's how designs get made. You take an existing game as a launching-off point, decide you want to try some variant of it, and build that. Comparison is normal, and it's said out loud.

Which makes the misalignment stranger. The whole craft runs on explicit reference points, and the reference point still doesn't survive the trip to the buyer. Bobby flagged a sharper edge too: when the two sides disagree about how novel a mechanic really is, the argument doesn't stay friendly. It turns into patent and intellectual property trouble.

Six — the feedback loop

Everything gets tested except the thing that's failing.

Tabletop has a serious feedback culture. It's aimed at a different problem.

What the testing is for

Playtesting optimizes for fun

Designers test relentlessly. Bobby laid out the machinery: playtest groups at conventions, Unpub for refining a design with strangers, Protospiel putting designers, testers and publishers in one room. It exists because friends give biased feedback and everyone knows it. The Op says Flip 7 was tested more than 1,000 times before print.

Source — The Op, "Flip 7: How did we get here?", Dec 2025
The outcome is fun. The outcome is not usually distinctiveness.Author Ryan Angus, in conversation

Once it's fun, it ships. Distinctiveness gets filed under marketing, which Ryan noted some designers avoid because they consider themselves artists. Bobby, who runs a company, was less forgiving: business is business.

Nobody in that room is a buyer

A Protospiel playtester is a peer. They show up fluent in mechanics, happy to sit through a rough prototype, genuinely interested in the problem.

The person at the Barnes & Noble shelf gives four seconds and no feedback. There's no playtest for a pitch that isn't landing, so the miss stays invisible until sales arrive, and then it just looks like a weak game.

The exception that proves the rule

Leder Games got Root onto shelf one

Root, designed by Cole Wehrle for Leder Games in 2018, is about as asymmetric as tabletop gets: four factions, four ways to win, effectively four rulebooks. Bobby raised it as the counterexample, a complicated game that reached a general audience anyway. It is stocked at Target now.

Wehrle has said he set out to make an accessible, interactive strategy game, easy to set up, easy to teach and easy to put away. The accessibility was designed in, not bolted on afterwards.

Sources — Leder Games, "Introducing Root"; Root (board game)

But look at what actually travelled

Root's illustrator, Kyle Ferrin, drew the four warring factions as woodland animals: cats, birds, mice, an armed raccoon. That art is what a buyer sees. Charming, legible, unlike anything else in the aisle. What they cannot see is the asymmetry, the genuinely novel part, which takes a full teach. Reviewers still warn casual groups off it.

Which is the paper's finding, playing out in public. Art was the one dimension where the two sides lined up, and art is what carried Root past the hobby shop. The mechanics didn't travel. They came in the box.

Seven — the industry

A craft industry that breaks the craft-industry rules.

Context from the call, running past what the paper claims.

How these markets usually work

A craft market, on paper

Author Cameron Verhaal studies cultural production markets, and he was the one who flagged how odd this result looks from that angle. Tabletop should behave like the others.

… in a lot of these other industries like craft beer or artisan cheese or farm to table restaurants … the producers and the consumers are often very much in lockstep in terms of the shared identity.Author Cameron Verhaal, in conversation

Cameron also studies what happens when those producers grow, and the pattern there is punishment. A cool product makes you successful, success brings growth, and the growth undermines what made you successful in the first place. He calls it the authenticity paradox.

… one of the biggest no-nos in craft beer is growing and selling out to (a global brewing conglomerate) or private equity …Author Cameron Verhaal, in conversation

An audience that won't hold still

The shared identity falls apart on contact. Kids' games, war games, party games, heavy strategy. One hobby in name, in practice a set of audiences with almost no overlap in what they've played. Cameron suspects that diversity is why this market pulls the two sides apart.

Tabletop also seems to skip the scaling penalty. His read: the hobby lost ground to video games, came back, and now treats a runaway hit as a win for everybody.

… they're just happy to get a win …Author Cameron Verhaal, in conversation

He was careful to mark that as a hunch. Untested, and an obvious next study.

Eight — the move

Say what you're different from.

The practical takeaway, and the two reasons it isn't free.

The claim on the box

Hand over the comparison

Author Ryan Angus framed the first half: point at a specific dimension instead of waving at the whole product.

… you can't just assume people are going to pick up on your cues.Author Ryan Angus, in conversation

Author Matt Barlow went further. Name the game you're measuring yourself against and the buyer has a foothold.

… if I can prompt you and say, this is like Settlers of Catan … now I get something in my mind.Author Matt Barlow, in conversation

But watch how it reads

Hobby buyers distrust producers who look like they're working an angle. Cameron Verhaal calls it a required disinterestedness: you're meant to be in it for love.

… consumers in this industry distrust producers who seem to behave strategically …Author Cameron Verhaal, in conversation

The paper leaves that tension open. It bites hardest in crowdfunding, where people commit before touching the product.

Now you've handed over the comparison set. They're standing where you're standing, and the one difference you spent two years on has somewhere to land. The cost: it reads as strategic, and this audience is suspicious of strategic.

The opposite strategy

Go far enough that comparison stops

Bobby's version: if you're taking a novel approach, change it enough that people latch onto it as its own thing. Halfway fails. A near-variant of a known game gets absorbed into that game.

Or make the game its own advertisement

Bobby pointed to Flip 7. Designed by Eric Olsen and launched at Gen Con 2024 through The Op, it was marketed by giving it away: a full promotional version, handed out, with the publisher's own retrospective describing thousands of copies gone and the giveaway still running. Nobody had to be told what it was like. Playing it was the pitch, which is positioning with a budget attached.

Nine — the point

Why it matters, on both sides.

Two consequences of one finding

For the research

Scholars usually measure distinctiveness from one side, often from archival data, and treat that number as the thing itself. If the two sides come apart this cleanly, a measure built from one isn't a stand-in for the other.

The authors push further. If a positioning strategy can't reliably be transmitted, the payoff from distinctiveness starts to look less like a choice and more like luck.

For the founder

A product can fail while being genuinely different, because the difference never arrived. From inside, that looks exactly like a product that wasn't different enough, and the two problems need opposite fixes.

Author Cameron Verhaal drew the line between this journal and a strategy one. A large firm mis-positions a product and absorbs it. For a venture, one placement decision can be the whole company.

… the stakes are just simply much higher for these entrepreneurs …Author Cameron Verhaal, in conversation
Digest — what kind of paper this is

Built to start an argument, not settle one

The team went question-driven on purpose: find an assumption nobody had checked, check it, watch it fail, then offer possible explanations instead of testing a hypothesis. All three are labeled conjecture. Cameron Verhaal was explicit that most papers chase an answer, while this one is trying to start a conversation. A follow-up is running on the producer side, where author Hyeonsuh Lee's early read is that designer behavior splits hard.

… there are a bunch of designers that do not care at all about market …Author Hyeonsuh Lee, in conversation
In plain terms

Most papers are a courtroom: here's my claim, here's my evidence. This one is closer to a phone call that opens with "something weird is going on, does anyone know why?"

Your product didn't sell. Which failure was it?

What went wrong

It wasn't different enough.

Then the fix is design

Push harder on a dimension. Take a real risk. Make the thing genuinely stranger and accept that some buyers will bounce off it. Expensive, slow, and correct only if the diagnosis is.

The difference never arrived.

Then the fix is transmission

The design was fine. Name your comparison set, point at the dimension that carries the novelty, and get the product into hands. Cheap next to a redesign, and wasted if you had the other problem.

The tell

Ask buyers what your product reminds them of. If they name something you didn't, or name nothing at all, you have a transmission problem, not a design problem.
